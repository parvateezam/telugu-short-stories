# The Two Who Argued Through Books

On Eluru Road in Vijayawada, there was an old bookshop called **Srinivasa Book Depot**.

Its owner, Subbarao, was sixty-eight years old. He liked talking to the people who came into his shop more than he liked selling them books.

One Sunday morning, around eleven, a young woman walked in.

“Uncle, do you have a book that can make someone less stubborn?”

Subbarao pushed his glasses up his nose.

“Your stubbornness or someone else's?”

“Someone else's.”

“Then shouldn't that person be reading it?”

“That's why I'm here.”

“Who's it for?”

“The man I'm going to marry.”

“Oh! In that case, my dear, you'll need patience before you need a book.”

The young woman laughed.

Her name was Madhavi. She was a Telugu lecturer at a college in Guntur.

Her fiancé, Raghu, was a civil engineer working in Chirala. Their families had arranged the match, but the two had been talking and getting to know each other for six months.

At first, everything had been fine.

Then the little arguments began.

Whatever Madhavi said, Raghu would look for the logic behind it.

“I love sitting by the sea,” she would say.

“The salty air will rust the bike,” he would reply.

“I feel like getting drenched in the rain.”

“You'll catch a cold.”

One day, thoroughly fed up, Madhavi asked him, “Raghu, have you ever done anything in your life without calculating the consequences?”

“I have. Once I got the figures wrong for a truckload of sand. Lost fifteen thousand rupees.”

From that day, Madhavi stopped arguing with him.

She started giving him books instead.

***

The first was **Barrister Parvateesam**, a classic Telugu comic novel about a young man's often foolish adventures as he tries to make his way in the world.

On the first page, she wrote:

*So you'll know that even people cleverer than you sometimes do foolish things.*

Raghu read it.

A week later, he gave her **Kanyasulkam**, a famous Telugu satirical play whose characters have been entertaining and provoking readers for generations.

Inside was a small slip of paper.

*If you keep looking for meaning in every word someone says, you'll find it hard to understand the person.*

Madhavi called him.

“Is this meant for me?”

“No. It's about Girisam.”

Girisam was the play's famously cunning, smooth-talking character.

“Liar.”

“Then the book has done its job.”

They both laughed.

From then on, they made an agreement.

Whenever they quarrelled, they wouldn't immediately send each other long, angry messages. Instead, one would give the other a book, with a sentence or two written on the first page.

The next time they met, they would talk about the book.

***

One day, Madhavi was an hour late reaching Vijayawada from Guntur.

Raghu was waiting at the bus stand, furious.

The following day, he sent her a book.

**The Value of Time.**

On the first page, he had written:

*So you'll understand how long an hour feels to someone waiting for you.*

Two days later, Madhavi sent him another book.

**Stories Mother Told Me.**

Inside, she wrote:

*So you'll learn to ask why someone is late before getting angry.*

Raghu called her.

“Sorry. Why were you late?”

“One of the girls at our college fell ill. I took her to the hospital.”

“You could have told me earlier.”

“I called you. You were angry and cut the call.”

Raghu was silent for a moment.

“Madhavi?”

“What?”

“Don't send me another book. I was wrong.”

***

After that, their arguments through books travelled all the way to the beach at Chirala.

One Sunday, they went to the shore at Vodarevu.

Madhavi took off her sandals and walked barefoot on the sand.

Raghu followed her, carefully holding the bike keys, his wallet and his phone.

“Raghu, look at the sea for once.”

“I am looking.”

“No, you're calculating how far the waves are coming in.”

“That's also a way of looking at it!”

Madhavi laughed.

“You know something? I'm a little afraid of marrying you.”

Raghu stopped.

“Why?”

“Because if you spend your whole life calculating everything, I'm afraid I'll have to spend mine dreaming alone.”

He said nothing.

The following week, Madhavi received a parcel.

Inside was **Amaravati Kathalu**, a celebrated collection of Telugu short stories about ordinary people and their lives in the Amaravati region.

On the first page, Raghu had written:

*I'm beginning to understand that not every story has a calculation behind it. Give me a little time.*

She read those words three times.

***

There were still two months to go before their wedding.

Then Madhavi received an excellent job offer in Ongole. A college principal's position.

Better pay. Greater responsibility. The opportunity she had wanted for years.

But Raghu had to remain in Chirala. All his construction projects were based there.

“If you go to Ongole, where will we live?” he asked.

“Somewhere in between.”

“Travelling every day will be difficult.”

“So should I give up the job?”

“I didn't say that.”

“But that's where all your calculations seem to lead.”

This time, the argument was serious.

They didn't speak for a week.

Madhavi went to Subbarao's bookshop in Vijayawada.

“Uncle, this time I don't know which book to give him.”

“Why, my dear?”

“We both want to be together. But neither of us wants to give up the life we've worked for.”

Subbarao thought for a while.

Then he reached beneath the counter and took out an old notebook.

“Read this.”

“Whose is it?”

“My wife's.”

Madhavi looked surprised.

“Did she write stories?”

“No. These are the household accounts from when we were newly married.”

Madhavi turned the pages.

Rice. Dal. Milk. Electricity bills.

On the last page was a sentence.

*We both earn. We both work hard. But each of us thinks our own struggles are greater.*

Madhavi looked up at him.

“What happened after that, Uncle?”

“She went to Tenali for her job. I stayed in Vijayawada.”

“For how long?”

“Seven years.”

“How did you manage?”

“She went and came back by bus every day, of course!”

Madhavi didn't laugh.

“Was it really that simple?”

“No. It was difficult. Very difficult. But compared with one of us losing a job, the bus journey seemed the smaller hardship.”

He paused.

“My dear, marriage isn't just about two people living under one roof. It's also about making room for both their lives.”

***

The following Sunday, Raghu came to Vijayawada.

Madhavi was waiting at Subbarao's bookshop.

“I've brought you a book,” she said.

Raghu smiled.

“So have I.”

“You go first.”

Raghu took some folded papers from his bag.

When he opened them, Madhavi saw a road map of Andhra Pradesh.

He had marked the road from Chirala to Ongole with a red pen.

Beside it, he had written:

*Your job is in Ongole. My work is in Chirala. Let's build our home somewhere in between.*

Madhavi's eyes filled with tears.

“When did you think of this?”

“The day after you stopped speaking to me.”

“Then why didn't you tell me?”

“I wanted to see which book you'd give me.”

Madhavi reached into her bag and took out a small diary.

Inside were the names of towns between Ongole and Chirala, details of houses available to rent and notes on travelling times.

Raghu looked at her in surprise.

“When did you do all this?”

“The day after you stopped speaking to me.”

They looked at each other and laughed.

From behind the counter, Subbarao called out:

“Ah, finally! After reading all those books, you've both arrived at the same calculation!”

***

They married on the auspicious date their families had chosen.

Subbarao arrived at the wedding carrying a large cardboard box.

“This is my present.”

Inside was a small wooden bookshelf.

A brass plate was fixed to it.

**MADHAVI & RAGHU**

*The Library of Arguments*

Below it was another inscription.

*Books need not be returned here. But conversations must always be resumed.*

Madhavi smiled.

“Uncle, why have you left so many shelves empty?”

“How would I know how many more arguments the two of you are going to have?”

Raghu immediately said, “Then you should have added another two shelves.”

Madhavi gave his hand a playful pinch.

***

A year after the wedding, Subbarao received a parcel.

It was from Madhavi and Raghu.

Inside was a book of baby names.

On the first page, they had written:

*Uncle, we've started arguing again.*

*If we have a daughter, Raghu wants to name her after his mother.*

*I want to name her after my grandmother.*

*We need your verdict.*

Subbarao read the note and burst out laughing.

His wife came in from the kitchen.

“What's making you laugh so much?”

He showed her the letter.

She read it.

Then she picked up a pen and wrote on the first page of the book:

*Don't start arguing over a name before the baby is even born. Once the child arrives, write both names on slips of paper and draw one. If you still don't like it, choose a third name!*

Subbarao read her reply and smiled.

“Janaki, you always want the last word.”

She looked at him.

“Is that why you've been arguing with me for fifty years?”

“No, that's not what I meant…”

“Then what?”

Subbarao thought for a moment.

Then he said quietly:

“If I stopped arguing with you, who else would I have to talk to?”

Janaki smiled.

“Then have your coffee first. We can argue afterwards.”

He took off his glasses and set them aside.

“All right. But don't put too little sugar in my coffee.”

“The doctor told you to cut down.”

“Is the doctor your husband, or am I?”

“That's exactly why I'm listening to him.”

Subbarao put his glasses back on.

“Even my bookshop doesn't have a book with an answer to that!”

**The End.**
